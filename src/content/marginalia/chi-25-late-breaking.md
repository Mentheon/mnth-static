A late-breaking work that turned up in the CHI '25 program caught my eye this month: *"What you don't add: subtractive design heuristics for therapeutic WebXR"* (Hossain et al.). It's only four pages, and most of it is anecdotal — but it pushes against a tendency I see in a lot of clinical XR work, including some of our own early sketches, which is to keep adding props until the room feels furnished.

The authors instead start with a bare scene and only restore the elements that participants in their formative studies asked for, by name. The headline finding is unsurprising and useful: people with cognitive load floors well below the typical young-adult-gamer baseline appreciated the missing things, not the present ones. Empty walls. No music. A ceiling fan that did not turn.

## Their three-part heuristic

- **Default to absence.** Every visual, audible, or interactive element should justify its inclusion against the cost of its noise floor. If you can't articulate the cost, leave it out.
- **Name the prop.** A useful environment is one where someone in the room could, if asked, point and say what something is *for* in plain language. Decoration is the failure mode.
- **Test for the stop.** The hardest evaluation is not "did the user reach the end?" but "did the user feel able to leave?". Their post-session interview script orients around this.

The middle one is the one I keep underlining. There's a lot of WebXR work that hides under the alibi of immersion, where any prop is a good prop because *the world feels real*. Hossain et al. push back on that fairly directly: a world that feels real but contains things the user can't articulate is a world that's working against them, not for them.

## Where I think it's thin

The paper undersells how hard "default to absence" is to operate inside a team. *Adding* a thing is easy to fund and ship; *removing* a thing requires someone to volunteer that their previous decision was wrong. There's no methodological glue here for that, and I'd love to see a follow-up that's mostly about the team practice, not the participant outcomes.

Still: a useful four pages. Worth circulating internally.
